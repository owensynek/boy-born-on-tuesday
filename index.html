The Boy Born on Tuesday

A two-child probability puzzle where one seemingly useless detail changes the answer.

The puzzle

A family has two children. At least one of them is a boy born on a Tuesday. What is the probability that both children are boys?

The answer \(\tfrac{13}{27}\) ≈ 48.1%
Not \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) 50%
And not \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) 33.3%

Assembled by Owen Synek using Claude Opus 5.5. Puzzle by Gary Foshee (2010). Visual approach from YATAQi and 3Blue1Brown. Full credits at the bottom.

01Probability as area

Before the puzzle, here is the tool that solves it. Draw every possibility as a square of area 1. A hypothesis takes up a slice of the square. Evidence keeps only part of each slice. After seeing the evidence, the probability of the hypothesis is its share of the area that is left.

\[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{\text{area of } H \text{ and } E}{\text{area of } E} = \frac{P(H)\,P(E \mid H)}{P(H)\,P(E \mid H) + P(\text{not } H)\,P(E \mid \text{not } H)} \]

The classic example: Steve is described as shy, tidy and detail-oriented. Is he more likely a librarian or a farmer? Most people say librarian. But there are about 20 farmers for every librarian. Even if the description fits 40% of librarians and only 10% of farmers, the farmers who fit still take up more area.

Fig. 1Librarian or farmer?

Visual and example: 3Blue1Brown, "Bayes theorem, the geometry of changing beliefs."

The Tuesday puzzle works exactly the same way. The hypothesis is "two boys", the evidence is "there is a Tuesday boy", and the square is the grid of all families.

02Warm-up: without the Tuesday

Each child is a boy or a girl, so there are four equally likely families. "At least one boy" rules out only girl-girl. Three cells remain, and one of them is boy-boy, so \(P = \tfrac{1}{3}\). Now split every child by day of the week too.

03The full sample space

Each child is one of 14 types: boy or girl, times 7 days. The x axis (columns) is the first child and the y axis (rows) is the second child. Step through the shading.

Fig. 2All 196 two-child families

There are \(14 \times 14 = 196\) equally likely families. Every cell has the same area, so probability is just counting cells.

\[ |\Omega| = 14 \times 14 = 196 \]

The clue keeps only families with a Tuesday boy: the Boy Tue column (14 cells) plus the Boy Tue row (14 cells). The gold cell, where both are Tuesday boys, sits in both, so it is counted once.

\[ |A| = 14 + 14 - 1 = 27 \]

Of those 27 cells, 13 are two-boy families: 7 in the column, 7 in the row, minus the shared cell.

\[ |BB \cap A| = 7 + 7 - 1 = 13 \]
\[ P(BB \mid A) = \frac{|BB \cap A|}{|A|} = \frac{13}{27} \approx 0.481 \]

Sample-space intersection visual: YATAQi, "The Most Controversial Puzzle in Probability."

04Why the extra detail matters

The overlap is the whole story. With two boys, there are two chances to have a Tuesday boy, but the family where both are Tuesday boys can only be counted once. That double-chance-minus-overlap tilts the count. The rarer the detail, the smaller the overlap, and the closer the clue gets to pointing at one specific child, which would give \(\tfrac{1}{2}\).

If the detail is shared by a fraction \(p\) of boys, the same counting gives

\[ P(BB \mid A) = \frac{2 - p}{4 - p} \]
Fig. 3How rare is the detail?
1 in 1 (just "a boy")1 in 365 (a birthday)
\(p = \) 1/7
\(P(BB \mid A) = \) 13/27 ≈ 0.4815

At 1 in 1 (just "a boy") you get 1/3. At 1 in 365 (a birthday) you get almost exactly 1/2.

05Another view: which families produce the clue?

Start with the four family types, each a quarter of all families. Each bar's height is the chance that a family of that type contains a boy with the detail. Two-boy families get two chances, minus the case where both boys have it. The answer is the two-boy share of the total shaded area. Move the slider above and watch the bars.

07One honest caveat

The answer depends on how the information reached you. If you specifically asked whether there is a Tuesday boy, the answer is 13/27. If the parent picked one child at random and told you about that child, you have learned about a specific child, and the answer is 1/2. Switch the simulation mode above to see both.
